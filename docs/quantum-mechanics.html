3.1 Entanglement

Prerequisites: vectors, absolute value

Quantum entanglement is defined as “the phenomenon of a group of particles being generated, interacting, or sharing spatial proximity in such a way that the quantum state of each particle of the group cannot be described independently of the state of the others, including when the particles are separated by a large distance”.

We will consider a potentially entangled system of two electrons. Electron spins are ‘quantised’, and are hence measured to be either ‘up’ or ‘down’ (see Stern-Gerlach experiment).

(For a more popular science-related analogy, consider a system of two Schrödinger’s cat set-ups. Here, the system is entangled if observing the state of one cat affects the state of the other cat. They are not necessarily both be the same state, or opposite states. Entanglement can just mean that the likelihood of the state of the second cat will shift if you observe the first cat, or vice versa. Likewise, in our system of two electrons, measuring the spin of one electron does not necessarily make the other electron measure the same or the opposite spin - it merely changes the probability of the states of the spin of the second electron.)

Let the down state be \(|0 \rangle\) and the up state be \(|1 \rangle\).

The full quantum spin state of an electron, \(|\psi_{1} \rangle\) can be expressed in the form below.

\[ \begin{equation} |\psi_{1} \rangle=a_{1}|0 \rangle+b_{1}|1 \rangle \end{equation} \]

The probability of the down state, given by the Born rule, is given by \(|a_{1}|^2\). Note that \(a_{1}\) can be a complex number.

Now consider two electrons.

\[ \begin{equation} |\psi_{1} \rangle=a_{1}|0 \rangle+b_{1}|1 \rangle \\ \left. |\psi_{2} \right\} = \left. a_{2}|0 \right\} + \left. b_{2}|1 \right\} \end{equation} \] The combined unentangled system, \(|\Psi \rangle\), is given by the so-called ‘tensor product’ of the two individual systems. Without formally defining the tensor product, it works in the way that you would expect when you think of it as simply multiplication. In the below calculations, in the two-numbered states, the first number refers to the spin of the first electron, and the second number refers to the spin of the second electron.

\[ \begin{equation} |\Psi \rangle = |\psi_{1}\rangle \otimes |\psi_{2}\rangle \\ = (a_{1}|0 \rangle+b_{1}|1 \rangle) \otimes (\left. a_{2}|0 \right\} + \left. b_{2}|1 \right\}) \\ = a_{1}a_{2}|00\rangle + a_{1}b_{2}|10\rangle + b_{1}a_{2}|01\rangle + b_{1}b_{2}|11\rangle \end{equation} \]

This means that a two-electron system that is entangled to any degree cannot be of the form above, even if the probabilities, as given by the Born rule, add up to 1. Such systems can be physically produced in a laboratory.^

As an example, take the so-called ‘singlet state’ below.

\[ \begin{equation} |sing \rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|10\rangle - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|01\rangle \end{equation} \]

One can easily verify that there could be no unique values for \(a_{1}, a_{2}, b_{1}, b_{2}\) that could produce the singlet state. Hence, we know that the singlet state must be entangled to some extent (in fact, it is maximally entangled). Susskind and Friedman (2015) also mention ways to calculate the degree of entanglement.

  • Sean Carroll, in his “Biggest Ideas in the Universe” video series gives an example of how entanglement occurs in a simple example. In short, a pion can decay into two photons with opposing spins, but it is unclear which spin one of the photons has until you observe the other.

One popular technique for creating entanglement in a laboratory setting is spontaneous parametric down-conversion.

3.2 Uncertainty principle

Prerequisites: linear algebra (eigenvalues, eigenvectors), complex numbers

In quantum mechanics, observable quantities are represented by ‘operators’. Matrices are an example of such operators, specifically in cases relating to observables that take discrete values such as energy. When a measurement is performed, the observed value of the underlying physical quantity effectively settles into one of its eigenvalues. Prior to the observation, the observable is in a superposition of its eigenvalues. The eigenvectors correspond to the possible states (‘eigenstates’) in which the system can be found with a definite value of that observable.

Operators \(\hat{A}\) and \(\hat{B}\) are said to ‘commute’ if \(\hat{A}\hat{B}−\hat{B}\hat{A}=0\). If \(\hat{A}\) and \(\hat{B}\) commute and are ‘Hermitian’ (each operator is equal to their conjugate transpose), then it can be shown that \(\hat{A}\) and \(\hat{B}\) share all their eigenvectors.* Note, however, that those eigenvectors are not necessarily associated with the same eigenvalue.

This means, if \(\hat{A}\) and \(\hat{B}\) commute and are Hermitian, if you measure both \(\hat{A}\) and \(\hat{B}\), firstly, you will reach an eigenvector that corresponds to one of the possible states of the system. This, in turn, corresponds to observing eigenvalues of both \(\hat{A}\) and \(\hat{B}\), which are generally different. These eigenvalues are the values that you observe for your physical quantities.

On the other hand, if \(\hat{A}\) and \(\hat{B}\) do not commute, their eigenvectors are generally not the same. If you measure one observable (say, A), the system collapses into one of the eigenvectors of A, which in general is a superposition of the eigenvectors of \(\hat{B}\). This is how the uncertainty principle arises. It is the reason why certain pairs of observables cannot be measured simultaneously, most famously position and momentum, but also other pairs like energy and time.

The expression \(\hat{A}\hat{B}−\hat{B}\hat{A}\) is known as the commutator. The key way in which the commutator is related to the Heisenberg uncertainty principle \(\sigma_{\hat{x}}\sigma_{\hat{p}}\geq\hbar/2\) can be seen in the “Robertson uncertainty relation”.

\[ \begin{equation} \sigma_{\hat{A}} \sigma_{\hat{B}} \geq \left| \frac{1}{2i} \langle [\hat{A}, \hat{B}] \rangle \right| \end{equation} \] Note that there is a stronger version of the above relation, known as the Robertson–Schrödinger uncertainty relation, as well as other strong related relations like the Maccone–Pati uncertainty relations.

Although commutation relations are important in typical formulations of quantum mechanics (and the broader quantum field theory), I should also mention that an alternative way to prove the uncertainty principle is using Fourier transform methods, a ubiquitous tool in applied mathematics. The momentum-space wave function is simply the Fourier transform of the position-space wave function. In short, as these two ‘spaces’ can be seen as two orthogonal basis vectors in a vector space, it is obvious that there can be no wave functions that are simultaneously localised in both position and momentum, which is exactly the definition of the Heisenberg uncertainty principle. Carroll (2024) has a short appendix on this idea and Fourier transforms in general.
